Intermolecular Forces Lab

Why does water boil at 100 °C while methane boils at −162 °C? It comes down to how strongly the molecules pull on each other.

The big idea: a tug-of-war

Molecules are always moving. Temperature measures how much motion energy they have. Molecules also attract each other. These attractions between separate molecules are called intermolecular forces (IMFs). Whether a substance is a solid, liquid, or gas depends on which side wins: motion pulling molecules apart or IMFs holding them together.

Key idea: Melting and boiling don't break molecules apart. The atoms inside each molecule stay bonded. Only the attractions between molecules are overcome. Stronger IMFs need more motion energy to overcome, so they need a higher temperature.

What makes the forces strong or weak?

There are three kinds of intermolecular forces. Every molecule has the first kind. Some molecules also have the second or third.

London dispersion

Every molecule · weak per contact, grows with size

Electrons are always moving. For an instant, more of them may be on one side. That makes a temporary dipole, which pushes the electrons in the next molecule over to shift the same way. The two molecules attract for that instant.

More electrons = stronger. A bigger electron cloud shifts more easily, so a big molecule like I₂ (106 e⁻) grips much harder than F₂ (18 e⁻). Long, stretched-out molecules also have more surface touching their neighbors.

Dipole–dipole

Polar molecules only · medium

When atoms with different electronegativities bond, electrons are shared unevenly. One end of the molecule is always slightly negative (δ−), the other slightly positive (δ+). This is a permanent dipole.

The δ+ end of one molecule attracts the δ− end of its neighbor. This adds extra grip on top of London dispersion.

Hydrogen bonding

H bonded to N, O, or F · strongest

When H is bonded to a very electronegative N, O, or F, the H is left almost bare: a very strong δ+. It is pulled hard toward a lone pair on an N, O, or F of a neighboring molecule.

It's an especially strong dipole–dipole attraction. Water can form up to four H-bonds per molecule, which is why it's so "sticky."

Heat it up: where does the energy go?

Turn on the heater and watch the thermometer. While a substance melts or boils, the temperature stops rising, even though you keep adding heat. That energy is being used to pull molecules away from each other, breaking IMFs, instead of making them move faster.

Same temperature, different forces

Put two substances at the same temperature. Their molecules have the same average motion energy, but they don't have the same grip on each other. That's why one can be a liquid while the other is already a gas.

IMFs give liquids their personality

Evaporation

Molecules at the surface that happen to be moving fast enough break free of their neighbors. Weaker IMFs → more molecules escape → faster evaporation and a higher vapor pressure.

Vapor pressure at 25 °C (mmHg)

Surface tension

Molecules at the surface are pulled inward by neighbors below them. Stronger IMFs → stronger inward pull → drops bead up instead of spreading flat. (Same-size drops on a waxy surface.)

Surface tension at 25 °C (mN/m)

Viscosity

To flow, molecules must slide past each other. Stronger IMFs → more "drag" → thicker liquid, so a ball sinks slower. Glycerol has three –OH groups that tangle up in H-bonds.

Summary: stronger IMFs mean…

PropertyStronger IMFsWhy
Melting pointHigher ↑More motion energy needed for molecules to break out of fixed positions.

Surface tensionHigher ↑Surface molecules are pulled inward harder.
ViscosityHigher ↑Molecules hold on to each other as they try to slide past.
