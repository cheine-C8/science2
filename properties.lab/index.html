Intensive vs. Extensive

Does it matter how much stuff you have? Cut it, weigh it, sink it, and heat it to find out.

Teacher notes
  • Timing: about 30 minutes total. Predict 3, Part 1 10, Part 2 7, Part 3 5, Part 4 5. Students who finish early can redo Part 1 with the second metal or try more beaker sizes.
  • Objective: students use data to show that extensive properties (mass, volume, energy) change with the amount of matter, and intensive properties (density, temperature, boiling point, color) do not. Then they use intensive properties to identify substances.
  • Standards: MS-PS1-2 / HS-PS1-3 (properties of substances); science practices: analyzing and interpreting data, constructing explanations.
  • Key misconception: many students predict that cutting a bar in half halves its density, or that mixing two 60 °C cups gives 120 °C. Parts 1 and 2 test both ideas directly.
  • Collecting work: the Report tab prints a one-page summary. "Save as PDF" works for uploading to an LMS. Recorded masses include small random measurement error, so every student's numbers are a little different.
  • Reset: the "Start over" button at the bottom of the Report tab clears this browser's saved work, which is useful on shared computers.

Part 1: Cut It Down

~10 min

Measure a metal bar, cut it in half, and measure again. Does the material change when you have less of it?

Lab bench

Procedure

  1. Choose a metal. Start with the whole bar.
  2. Place it on the balance to measure its mass.
  3. Lower it into the cylinder. The water starts at 100.0 mL. The water rises by the sample's volume (1 mL = 1 cm³).
  4. Click Record trial. In the data table, calculate volume = final − initial water level, then density = mass ÷ volume.
  5. Cut the sample in half and repeat. Record at least 3 different sizes of one metal and at least 1 trial of the other metal.

Two kinds of physical properties

Intensive propertyDoes not depend on the amount of matter. A drop of water and a swimming pool both boil at 100 °C.
Extensive propertyDoes depend on the amount of matter. Two bars have twice the mass of one bar.
