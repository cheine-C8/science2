The Viscosity Lab

Viscosity is a liquid's resistance to flowing. Molecules in a liquid have to slide past each other to flow. The more strongly they stick to their neighbors (intermolecular forces), and the bigger and more tangled they are, the harder that sliding gets. Pick a substance and watch.

The big idea

Stronger and more numerous attractions between molecules → higher viscosity. Size matters too: big molecules have more surface for London dispersion forces and can tangle like spaghetti. Temperature works against all of this, since faster-moving molecules escape their neighbors more easily.

Think about it

1. Water and honey both make hydrogen bonds. Why is honey about 10,000 times thicker?

A water molecule is tiny. Honey is mostly sugar (glucose and fructose), and each sugar molecule has five O–H groups. That means one sugar molecule can hydrogen-bond to many neighbors at once, so it is held in a sticky web. Bigger molecules also have stronger dispersion forces.

2. Why does honey get runny when you warm it in the microwave?

Higher temperature means more kinetic energy. The sugar molecules move fast enough to break their hydrogen bonds more often, so they can slide past each other more easily.

3. Silly Putty bounces and pitch shatters. How can they also be liquids?

Their molecules need time to slide past each other. A hammer hit is too fast, so the material acts like a solid. Given minutes (putty) or years (pitch), the molecules slowly let go and rearrange, and it flows.

4. Pitch molecules have no hydrogen bonds. What holds them together?

London dispersion forces. These are weak for small molecules, but pitch molecules are huge flat clusters of carbon rings that stack together. Added up over a giant surface, dispersion forces become very strong.
